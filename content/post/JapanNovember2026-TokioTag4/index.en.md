From a meditative morning in Nakano's quiet temple gardens and an unexpected dose of German nostalgia inside a Japanese railway museum to Guinness World Record-breaking light displays across Shinjuku's skyscraper canyons: day four of our trip showed off Tokyo at its finest, most multifaceted best.

A day stuffed with oddball discoveries: 200 millstones laid to eternal rest, autumn harvest work with a pungent ginkgo aroma, a Brandenburg Gate beneath Tokyo's railway viaducts, electromechanical relay nostalgia, and an evening fruit tea along the Sumida River unexpectedly accompanied by flashing red police lights, searchlights, and patrol boats—before wrapping up in the quiet glow of Sensō-ji at night.

<!--more-->

## Quiet Morning in Nakano: The 200 Millstones of Hōsen-ji

Having checked into our new base in Asakusa the day before, we kicked off the morning nice and easy far from the big tourist crowds. The Ginza and Marunouchi lines carried us straight across town to **Nakano-sakaue** Station. Our morning stroll targeted two historic temples that barely rate a footnote in most guidebooks—and unfairly so.

First stop: the spacious Shingon temple **Hōsen-ji** (宝仙寺). The moment you step onto the grounds, you're wrapped in the kind of deep stillness that feels almost impossible in the middle of a global metropolis. Rising above raked gravel, an elegant three-story pagoda pierces a flawlessly blue morning sky.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag4/PXL_20261112_010432717.jpg" alt="Elegant three-story pagoda at Hōsen-ji Temple in Nakano beneath a bright blue sky" width="600" height="1067" >}}

Hōsen-ji's true historical curiosity, however, sits right at the foot of the pagoda: a sizable mound built out of hundreds of stacked stone mills—the **Ishiusuzuka** (石臼塚, Millstone Mound).

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag4/PXL_20261112_010553606.jpg" alt="The Ishiusuzuka millstone mound at Hōsen-ji with a water basin in front of the three-story pagoda" width="600" height="1067" >}}

During the Edo and Meiji eras, water power from the nearby Kanda River drove countless mills; Nakano was the buckwheat flour (*soba*) hub for the entire region. When modern machinery took over in the early 20th century and the old hand-carved basalt millstones retired, nobody simply tossed them onto a scrap heap.

Out of gratitude for generations of faithful service, the temple's head priest at the time bought up over 200 decommissioned millstones and erected a monument to console their "souls" (*kuyō*). Today, the mound doubles as an artistic water feature: water trickles out of the millstone spouts into a clear basin—a textbook example of the Japanese philosophy of showing respect even to everyday tools.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag4/PXL_20261112_011504821.jpg" alt="Main hall of Hōsen-ji Temple in warm mid-morning light" width="1200" height="675" >}}

## Jōgan-ji: A *Lost in Translation* Filming Location and the Aroma of Ginkgo Harvest

Just a few minutes' walk further south waited our next stop: the Zen temple **Jōgan-ji** (成願寺). Film and pop-culture buffs may recognize its distinctive architecture—the white gatehouse with its red balcony in *Ryūgū-mon* (Dragon Palace Gate) style served as the backdrop for Scarlett Johansson's rainy, melancholic temple walk in Sofia Coppola's 2003 film *Lost in Translation*.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag4/PXL_20261112_014433219.jpg" alt="Distinctive white Ryūgū-mon-style entrance gate of Jōgan-ji Temple in Nakano" width="600" height="1067" >}}

Jōgan-ji showed itself at its absolute finest today: completely open, peaceful, and free of tour-bus crowds, basking in warm morning sunlight. The main hall (*Daiōhōden*), with its impressive wood carvings and sweeping roofline, radiated a serene grandeur.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag4/PXL_20261112_015013150.jpg" alt="The venerable main hall Daiōhōden of Jōgan-ji Temple in sunny morning light" width="1200" height="675" >}}

The real highlight of the visit, though, was a quintessentially Japanese autumn ritual that engaged every single sense at once: the annual harvest of **ginkgo nuts** (*ginnan*, 銀杏).

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag4/PXL_20261112_015858836.jpg" alt="Fallen ginkgo fruits caught in a wide green net stretched beneath the tree" width="1200" height="675" >}}

Beneath a towering ginkgo tree still wearing its late-summer green, a giant green net was strung up like a colossal hammock to catch falling fruit. Anyone who has ever walked past a fruiting ginkgo tree knows the score: the fleshy outer pulp gives off an unmistakable, nose-curling stench of rancid butyric acid.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag4/PXL_20261112_015912321.jpg" alt="Macro shot of two fresh ginkgo fruits resting in green mesh netting against the sky" width="1200" height="675" >}}

At the temple, this pungent business was handled with practiced routine: the soft outer flesh gets scrubbed away thoroughly to reveal the pale, hard-shelled nuts inside. On several tables stood shallow wooden and mesh trays where the cleaned nuts were left to sun-dry in the midday warmth.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag4/PXL_20261112_020134815.jpg" alt="The huge ginkgo tree at Jōgan-ji with its harvest net, drying trays, and strict no-smoking sign" width="600" height="1067" >}}

Once dried, they are cracked and roasted—a prized autumn snack with beer or a savory delicacy tucked into dishes like *chawanmushi* egg custard. A sign pinned to the trunk also warned unambiguously against smoking (禁煙)—hardly surprising given the prized harvest and historic wooden buildings!

## Bizarre Find at the Tobu Museum: The Brandenburg Gate Under the Tracks

From Nakano, we rode metro and rail across northeastern Tokyo to Sumida, bound for the **Tobu Museum** (東武博物館) right at *Higashi-Mukōjima* Station. The museum has a great architectural quirk: it tucks directly beneath the elevated viaduct of the Tobu Skytree Line, meaning the low rumble of real trains echoes through the ceiling every couple of minutes.

The biggest surprise of the day, however, hit us inside the massive model-railway hall: as your eyes wander across accurately modeled viaducts, Shinkansen tracks, and Japanese suburban stations, you suddenly do a double take.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag4/PXL_20261112_033312825.jpg" alt="Large model railway diorama at the Tobu Museum featuring a detailed miniature Brandenburg Gate" width="1200" height="675" >}}

Standing right in the middle of the layout in crisp miniature architecture is: the **Brandenburg Gate**! Columns, Quadriga, cobblestone plaza, and tiny European compact cars parked out front, neatly labeled *ブランデンブルク門* (Brandenburg Gate). And as if that weren't enough German idyll for a Tokyo train diorama, the tracks loop right past a complete fortified town wall of **Rothenburg ob der Tauber**—half-timbered houses, city gate, tiled roofs, and all.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag4/PXL_20261112_033547495.jpg" alt="Miniature replica of the medieval half-timbered town of Rothenburg ob der Tauber in the diorama" width="1200" height="675" >}}

Once you've digested that delightful slice of Germany's Romantic Road tucked inside Sumida's rail network, the museum serves up plenty of serious historical engineering: beautifully restored commuter cars from Tobu's early days, like the 1924 Deha 1 railcar with polished mahogany paneling, gleaming ceiling lamps, and royal-blue velvet benches.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag4/PXL_20261112_033927604.jpg" alt="Interior of a historic wooden Tobu commuter train car with blue velvet seats and leather grab straps" width="600" height="1067" >}}

For telecom and networking hearts, the museum held a particular treat: a glass-fronted cabinet housing an electromechanical **crossbar telephone exchange** (クロスバー部) that once handled the railway's internal phone traffic. Hundreds of relay coils, featherlight armatures, and kilometers of neatly laced wiring harnesses—analog telecommunications in its purest engineering glory.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag4/PXL_20261112_035133300.jpg" alt="Fully intact historic crossbar relay switchboard cabinet for railway telephone switching" width="600" height="1067" >}}

And of course there was hands-on fun too: an actual train cab console complete with chunky brake handle, master controller, speedometer, and driver's seat from which you could drive real model trains around an enclosed track layout equipped with working ATS (Automatic Train Stop) signals.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag4/PXL_20261112_035651576.jpg" alt="At the original control desk of the train simulator in the Tobu Museum with throttle levers and driver's seat" width="600" height="1067" >}}

## Slowing Down at Mukōjima-Hyakkaen: Edo Garden and Autumn Macros

Right around the corner from the museum, the afternoon served up the ideal counterbalance to all that heavy hardware: **Mukōjima-Hyakkaen** (向島百花園), a historic botanical garden dating back to the late Edo period.

An originally planned quick detour to neighboring **Renge-ji** Temple (真言宗智山派 清瀧山 蓮花寺, historically known as *Terashima Daishi*, one of the famed "Edo San Daishi") unfortunately fell through: construction work was in full swing across the temple grounds, leaving most of it fenced off. All the nicer, then, to slip straight into the peaceful sanctuary of the garden next door.

Originally laid out by wealthy merchants and literati, this garden deliberately skips the rigid geometry and artificial hills of feudal lord estates—here, seasonal wildflowers and blossoms take center stage.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag4/PXL_20261112_054618789~2.jpg" alt="Macro shot of a hoverfly feeding on nectar atop a bright pink autumn blossom" width="1000" height="900" >}}

In the warm late-afternoon glow, the garden served up gorgeous photo ops: late chrysanthemums drew busy hoverflies and bees, while on a pale pink blossom an immaculate Asian comma butterfly (*Polygonia c-aureum*) sunned itself, its scalloped wings glowing gold in the backlight.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag4/PXL_20261112_054731620.jpg" alt="An Asian comma butterfly resting with spread wings on a pink chrysanthemum" width="600" height="1067" >}}

Scattered among bamboo groves and reeds sit relics of centuries past—like a traditional pulley well (*tsurube-ido*) complete with wooden roof, bucket, and woven bamboo cover. A wonderfully quiet spot to catch your breath before the evening city rush.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag4/PXL_20261112_063213405.jpg" alt="Traditional Japanese pulley well with wooden rope wheel and bucket at Mukōjima-Hyakkaen" width="600" height="1067" >}}

## Shinjuku's Colossal Wall of Light: TOKYO Night & Light at City Hall

As twilight crept in, we headed across to Nishi-Shinjuku. There are certain places in Tokyo you simply have to revisit every single trip—and the twin monolithic towers of the **Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building** (東京都庁, another Kenzō Tange masterpiece) have been on my personal must-visit list for years.

Since this year, though, there's a record-breaking spectacle running across them: **"TOKYO Night & Light."**

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag4/PXL_20261112_091301162.NIGHT.jpg" alt="Monumental TOKYO Night & Light architectural projection on the Shinjuku Metropolitan Government Building towers" width="600" height="1067" >}}

Spanning an astonishing **13,904 square meters**, the installation officially holds the Guinness World Record for the largest permanent architectural projection mapping display on Earth. From the vast, sunken Citizens' Plaza below, you crane your neck upward as gray concrete and glass ignite in luminous cobalt blue. Sweeping projections of crescent moons, bridge arches, traditional patterns, and futuristic waves ripple across hundreds of meters of facade, tightly synced to booming orchestral music.

Hundreds of spectators lounge casually across the steps and artificial turf—a completely free, technically jaw-dropping open-air show right in the middle of the skyscraper business district.

## Nightcap in Asakusa: Flashing Red Sirens on the River and a Quiet Sensō-ji

Late at night, the Ōedo and Ginza lines whisked us back to Asakusa. Before calling it a day, one last cozy stop remained: at *Tully's Coffee* right by Azuma Bridge, I grabbed a steaming cup of **&TEA Cabernet & Fruit Tea** (*カベルネ＆フルーツティー*)—paired with a prime panoramic view across the river, where Tokyo Skytree glowed deep purple and dinner boats glided across the dark Sumida.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag4/PXL_20261112_105547273.jpg" alt="Night view across the Sumida River toward the purple-lit Tokyo Skytree and a brightly lit boat" width="1200" height="675" >}}

While I sipped hot fruit tea and started a timelapse of the river traffic, things suddenly turned unexpectedly dramatic:

Up on the bridge (at 35°42'51.1"N, 139°48'12.3"E), several police cruisers pulled up with their signature rotating red warning lights (*sekishokutō*). Moments later, two police boats emerged on the dark river. Sweeping blinding searchlights tirelessly across the water and steep seawalls, they blasted urgent Japanese announcements over their loudspeakers again and again. From a distance there was no telling what sparked the operation—yet the nocturnal scene of crimson light reflections dancing across the waves beneath the calm giant of Skytree couldn't have been more suspenseful.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag4/PXL_20261112_111029814.LONG_EXPOSURE-01.jpg" alt="Long exposure of the dramatically lit main hall of Sensō-ji Temple in nighttime stillness" width="1200" height="675" >}}

The perfect, peaceful closing chapter to this eventful day came with one final walk across the grounds of **Sensō-ji**. Once the souvenir stalls of Nakamise-dōri have long pulled down their shutters and the daytime tourists are tucked into bed, Tokyo's oldest temple belongs entirely to the night.

Floodlights bathe the monumental main hall (*Hondō*) in solemn crimson and gold. In a long exposure, the handful of remaining night owls blur into ghostly streaks before the massive timber colossus. A moment of utter stillness—and the finest possible contrast after 12 hours of nonstop surprises across this inexhaustible metropolis.
