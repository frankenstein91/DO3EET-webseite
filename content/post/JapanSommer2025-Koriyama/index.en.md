When I stepped off the Shinkansen on the morning of August 28, I had no real itinerary—just a vague urge to explore a city most travelers treat as little more than a transfer stop. First impressions around the station matched the standard picture of a busy modern Japanese hub. Kōriyama is a "core city" of Fukushima Prefecture, an important crossroads for commerce and rail, and you feel that immediately. Still, I had a hunch there was more hiding behind the concrete-and-glass facade. Armed with curiosity and zero fixed plans, I set off on foot to peel back the city's layers, starting right at its bustling modern heart.

## A Walk Through Time and Nature
My instincts (the new codename for Google Maps) steered me away from the main boulevards and into a maze of side streets where the neighborhood character slowly began to shift. The station-area rush faded into a quieter, almost village-like calm. It turned into a long walk, carrying me steadily southwest for over an hour past tidy homes and small local shops.

### In the Realm of Dancing Jewels: The Old Toyoda Reservoir
Suddenly the houses opened up, revealing a vast, seemingly forgotten expanse of green. I was standing in front of 旧豊田浄水場貯水池, the old water reservoir of the Toyoda filtration plant. What stretched out before me wasn't a manicured park, but a piece of land nature had reclaimed with full force. Lush grasses and reeds swayed in the breeze, and the air hummed with a soft buzz and whir.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanSommer2025-Koriyama/PXL_20250828_080557224.PANO.jpg" alt="" width="1000" height="1000" >}}

The reason for this wildness is fascinating: the site—a whopping 88,000 square meters—has sat idle for over a decade. Political and urban-planning indecision over what to build here produced a stalemate that turned into an accidental blessing for wildlife. While city hall and local politicians debated putting up new buildings—often derisively dubbed *hakomono* ("box architecture") in Japan—their productive procrastination inadvertently grew a thriving biotope.

As I stepped closer to the water, I caught my breath. The air wasn't merely humming—it was dancing. Countless dragonflies, hundreds or maybe thousands of them, darted like iridescent arrows above the water, hunting, mating, and perching on reed stalks. In late summer, peak season for many dragonfly species in Japan, I had stumbled straight into a genuine natural spectacle.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanSommer2025-Koriyama/PXL_20250828_081018705.jpg" alt="" width="1000" height="1000" >}}

What I witnessed here was the happy outcome of geology meeting bureaucracy. The soil of the former reservoir is clay-rich earth—fantastic at holding water, yet a nightmare for construction, requiring expensive pilings and ground stabilization. That exact trait makes it tailor-made for wetlands and habitats. My unplanned detour felt like casting an accidental vote in the local debate over the site's future. Kōriyama now actually plans to turn the grounds into an official nature and education park with boardwalks and wetland habitats—a formal nod to the value created here purely by leaving things alone. Those dancing dragonflies were living proof that sometimes the smartest human intervention is doing nothing at all.

## From Pop Culture to Quiet Observation
The following day, August 29, sharpened Kōriyama's contrasts even further. My path led into sprawling Kaiseizan Park, a green lung in the city that hosts two completely different—yet equally fascinating—facets of modern Japanese culture.

### The Lucky Park at the Heart of Town

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanSommer2025-Koriyama/PXL_20250828_084048510.jpg" alt="" width="1000" height="1000" >}}

Right inside Kaiseizan Park, I stumbled upon a scene of pure joy and playfulness: ラッキー公園, Lucky Park. From afar you couldn't miss the centerpiece—a giant six-meter slide shaped like the Pokémon Chansey, known in Japan as "Lucky." Surrounded by playground equipment modeled after other Pokémon like Blissey, Pikachu, and Lickitung, kids and families were having a blast.  
This park is far more than just a playground, though. It's part of a thoughtful, heartwarming collaboration between Fukushima Prefecture and The Pokémon Company. Following the 2011 triple disaster, the region looked for ways to bring joy back to locals—especially children—and reshape its image in a positive light. Chansey was appointed Fukushima's official "Support Pokémon" for a brilliant reason: its Japanese name, "Lucky" (ラッキー), symbolizes good fortune, and the name "Fukushima" (福島) literally begins with the kanji for luck or blessing (福, *fuku*).

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanSommer2025-Koriyama/PXL_20250828_085957478.jpg" alt="" width="1000" height="1000" >}}

Opened in March 2022, Kōriyama's park is one of several across the prefecture—a masterclass in strategic rebranding that harnesses Pokémon's global pull to tell a fresh, uplifting story of resilience, hope, and good fortune. To a casual visitor like me, it looks like a cheerful, colorful park. Beneath the surface, it stands as a powerful symbol of an entire region choosing to shape its future with a smile.

Even the mobile game Pokémon GO ties right in: wild Chansey spawn more frequently here, and the playground structures double as in-game PokéStops. Why am I even mentioning that game?... Simple: I've been banned from it for years for GPS spoofing.

### Kōriyama and the Radiation Monitors
As I wandered around town, something kept catching my eye that initially felt a bit jarring: small solar-powered monitoring stations continuously flashing current ambient radiation levels on digital readouts. As a ham radio operator, gadgets like that immediately piqued my technical curiosity. I pulled out my phone and snapped photos of plenty of displays—numbers I recall seeing included 0.146 µSv/h and 0.081 µSv/h.  
In a region linked around the world with the name Fukushima, numbers like those could look alarming without context. For technically minded visitors like me, however, they offer a fascinating chance to put things in perspective. These ubiquitous monitors aren't a cause for alarm—they're an exercise in radical transparency. In a post-2011 world where public trust is priceless, authorities chose not to bury the data, instead weaving it into everyday streetscapes as casually as a public clock or thermometer.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanSommer2025-Koriyama/PXL_20250828_075530193.jpg" alt="" width="1000" height="1000" >}}
{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanSommer2025-Koriyama/PXL_20250829_030212483.LONG_EXPOSURE-01.COVER.jpg" alt="" width="1000" height="1000" >}}

Not only is radiation in Kōriyama completely harmless, it **actually sits near the lower end of what counts as normal back in Germany**, and is orders of magnitude lower than the cosmic dose you pick up on a long-haul flight. Extrapolated over a full year, 0.146 µSv/h works out to roughly 1.28 mSv—well within the global range of natural background radiation of 1 to 10 mSv per year. In that sense, these stations are a powerful public-education tool, carrying on a quiet, nonstop dialogue with residents and visitors alike: *"We're monitoring the environment, we're sharing the numbers openly with you, and it's safe."*

## A Sea of Lights From a Bird's-Eye View
As dusk settled in, I headed back toward the station to the city's unmistakable landmark: the 24-story "Big Eye" tower. My final photo of the day carries a timestamp of 20:01 local time, meaning I must have squeezed onto the observation deck just under the wire before the 20:00 closing bell.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanSommer2025-Koriyama/PXL_20250829_083737729.jpg" alt="" width="1000" height="1000" >}}

The upper floors (20 through 24) house the "Koriyama City Fureai Science Center 'Space Park,'" a science museum boasting what Guinness World Records recognizes as the highest planetarium on Earth. My target, though, was the observation deck on the 22nd floor, 96 meters up—which, happily, is completely free to enter.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanSommer2025-Koriyama/PXL_20250829_085155065.jpg" alt="" width="1000" height="1000" >}}

The view waiting up there was breathtaking and the ideal cap to my exploration. Below me, Kōriyama fanned out as a glittering sea of light. From up high, all the puzzle pieces of the past day and a half snapped together into one coherent picture. I could trace the railway tracks that brought me into town; off in the distance, make out the dark, sprawling expanse of Kaiseizan Park hiding cheerful Lucky Park; and gaze toward where the wild, dragonfly-filled reservoir lay. Exploring at street level had been a collection of close-ups and fleeting moments; looking down from above was the synthesis.  
It was that moment when the map becomes the territory, and individual stops along my walk merged into the identity of a single, fascinating city.
