# Preface
A new trend is emerging in Japan's tourism sector: starting in early July 2025, numerous tourist attractions—and possibly even public transit—are set to introduce a dual-pricing policy. Since this would run alongside the already common mandatory accommodation/culture taxes, I'm not yet sure how it will all play out in practice. Still, in what is likewise a free-market economy, I assume this will also open the door for other businesses to adopt dual pricing.

# The Government's Rationale
Announced by the Japanese government, this measure aims to manage growing overtourism, generate funds to preserve cultural heritage, and improve tourism infrastructure—especially given the recent surge in visitor numbers fueled by the weak yen.

# What Might Stand in the Way?
Japan's Antimonopoly Act plays a central role in regulating unfair trade practices, including price discrimination. Unreasonable price discrimination based on region or customer appears to be unlawful under this framework. At the same time, it may not apply here since it primarily targets competition between businesses—and "unreasonable" doesn't seem clearly defined in my view either. One could still argue that dual pricing unfairly disadvantages foreign consumers, especially when the attractions are considered essential or unique. Let's see what decisions end up being made here.

The Act against Unjustifiable Premiums and Misleading Representations also sounds quite relevant to this topic. This law aims to protect consumers from unfair and deceptive representations, including in pricing. While dual pricing itself isn't explicitly banned, it could be viewed as an unfair representation—specifically as a "misleading advantage." If the tourist price is framed as the standard price and locals receive a genuine discount based on residency, that framing might be less likely to count as misleading, meaning it wouldn't be an obstacle after all... Well, crap.

Although the constitution provides a general principle of equality, its direct applicability to dual pricing for tourists based on nationality is uncertain. The lack of explicit protection against discrimination based on nationality in the constitution is a key point. Article 14's focus on "race" may not automatically extend to nationality in every interpretation. Court cases involving refusal of service based on foreign nationality—though not directly about pricing—do point to a growing legal awareness of discrimination against foreigners in Japan.

# The German-Japanese Treaty of Friendship
The foundation of official relations is the Treaty of Friendship, Commerce and Navigation signed between Prussia and Japan on January 24, 1861. Germany's Federal Foreign Office confirms the 1861 treaty between Prussia and Japan as that foundation. The German Embassy in Japan noted its 160th anniversary in 2021, and the German Federal Government likewise referenced this treaty in the context of 160 years of relations. The Foreign Office emphasizes the close partnership built on shared values such as democracy and free trade, and the German government's 2023 joint declaration with Japan highlights shared values and a commitment to free and fair trade. Since I can't imagine dual pricing being politically viable in Germany, I don't think it aligns with these shared treaties and principles either.

Both countries are also members of the WTO, which likewise promotes non-discrimination through MFN (Most-Favoured-Nation) principles. Even if the historic treaty doesn't explicitly forbid modern dual pricing, the spirit of today's relations and international trade norms could still be raised in discussions or diplomatic channels. If the German-Japanese Treaty of Friendship contains a broader MFN clause than the WTO's, charging German tourists higher attraction prices than Japanese residents could be argued to violate that clause. The decisive factor would be how "commerce" is interpreted and whether access to tourist attractions falls under that definition within the treaty's context.
