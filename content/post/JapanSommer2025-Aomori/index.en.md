So there I was again. Aomori. Two full days plus arrival and departure, wedged between the rumble of trains and the scent of salt water. It was my second time in this city at the northern tip of Honshu, and to be completely honest, I carried a quiet worry in the back of my mind. My first visit had been a few years back in the dead of winter, when the world lay buried beneath a merciful, meter-thick blanket of snow. And I thought it was gorgeous. Snow in Aomori isn't just frozen water; it's the finest makeup artist on the planet—a soft-focus filter that smooths every rough edge, hushes every sound, and wraps the town in an aura of timeless beauty.

Now, in late summer, I stood face-to-face with Aomori without its makeup on. And yes, I stand by my controversial take: I prefer the city in winter. Snow politely covers up some of the architectural scars that come with a town that had to reinvent itself almost from scratch after the war. Roughly 88% of Aomori was destroyed in 1945, and what went up afterward was modern, functional, and built with an honest, no-frills aesthetic. In summer, you see all of it—including a fair few chipped corners and rough patches.  
It's a bit like seeing a close friend without his sharp winter coat for the first time—still the same great guy, except you suddenly notice he's wearing a slightly questionable T-shirt. Yet as I would come to learn, maybe that exact honesty was the true charm of our second meeting.

## The Daily Ritual: My Culinary Evening Pilgrimage, MOS Burger
Every traveler has their rituals. Some meditate at sunrise; others keep a journal. I go to MOS Burger. Every single evening. Without exception. My home base in Aomori was the MOS Burger in Yanakawa, and that spot quickly became **more than just a burger joint**. It was my anchor, my lighthouse in the culinary surf, my dependable friend at the end of a long day packed with new impressions.

Call me an **uncultured philistine** if you must. Here I am in a prefecture famous for impossibly fresh seafood and legendary apples, and what do I do? Make a nightly pilgrimage to a fast-food chain. But MOS Burger isn't just any chain. Every burger is cooked to order; you get a little number and wait reverently until the freshly seared patty, crisp onions, and iconic tomato meat sauce unite into a minor masterpiece.  

Still, the real reason for my daily pilgrimage—the true object of my quest—wasn't even the burger. It was **dessert**. Every single time, **dessert** blew me away. One evening it was the *アイスドルチェ カップ ショコラ風ムースケーキ*, a chocolatey mousse cake in a cup whose creamy density could silence any philosopher. Another night I treated myself to the *プレミアムスフレパンケーキ バターで味わう ふわしゅわスフレ＜ハニーメープル＆ホイップ＞*—a heavenly, cloud-fluffy soufflé pancake with butter, honey-maple syrup, and whipped cream that, as connoisseurs note, leans closer to delicate cake than pancake. A flawless, dependable finale to days full of adventure. My delicious little secret in the heart of Aomori.

## Apple Fever
To morally offset my nightly **escapades** at MOS Burger, I put myself on a strict daytime regimen—a diet consisting exclusively of Aomori's most sacred commodity: the apple. I drank the famous apple juice like there was no tomorrow. I ate apples in every shape and color straight out of hand. I indulged in a slice of apple pie so juicy it practically collapsed under its own weight. It was a form of penance, an attempt to make peace with the soul of the prefecture.

And the more I consumed, the deeper it sank in that apples here aren't merely fruit. They are a religion—the product of a stubborn, unyielding passion locals call **Joppari**. It's that fierce grit that drives farmers out into chest-deep January snow to prune branches, **hand-pollinate every single blossom**, thin out young fruit so the remaining ones grow larger, and **turn every single apple by hand so it catches sunlight from all sides**. Word is it takes 223 hours of labor just to tend 10 apple trees.  

Wide temperature swings between day and night make the apples grow during the day and pack in sugars at night to protect against the chill—a clever antifreeze trick that leaves them outrageously sweet and juicy. And the sheer variety of products spun out of these natural wonders is practically endless.  
I tried to become Aomori itself, one bite and one sip at a time.

## The High Art of Nokkedon
If my evenings at MOS Burger embodied pure comfort and predictability, lunch at the Aomori Gyosai Center was the exact opposite: an exciting, tactical adventure. Waiting inside was a major highlight of the trip: *nokkedon*. The concept is brilliantly simple yet endlessly deep. You buy a booklet of tickets—essentially the currency/food tokens for this culinary game—find a stall flying an orange flag to score a bowl of steaming rice, and then head out among the dark-blue-flagged stalls to draft your own toppings.

For me there was only one mission, a single target that eclipsed every other temptation—sea urchin, scallops, grilled meats: tuna. Across every grade of fat. I was a specialist on a sacred crusade. The market was a labyrinth of aromas, clatter, and vendors shouting in charming Tsugaru dialect. I stalked the aisles like a hunter, eyes locked on the varying shades of red across the *maguro* cuts. One ticket for *chūtoro*, the medium-fatty tuna. Two tickets for *ōtoro*, that glorious fatty belly that melts on the tongue like butter. Another ticket for *akami*, the lean, ruby-red loin. I engineered a bowl that was an homage to one fish alone—a symphony in tuna (plus a splash of cephalopod).

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanSommer2025-Aomori/PXL_20250827_035328992.jpg" alt="" width="1000" height="1000" >}}

The experience was the perfect antithesis to MOS Burger. There, everything was fixed; here, every choice was mine. There, I sat passively waiting; here, I was the active architect of my own happiness. It was the ultimate form of bespoke dining, a dialogue between me, the market, and the bounty of the sea. Sitting in the little rest corner devouring my custom-built tuna tower, I knew: *this* is Aomori.

## Cultural Heavyweights
Aomori's waterfront isn't just a hub for ferries and fishing boats—it's also a kind of open-air museum of the city's soul. Two of my biggest stops sat just a few minutes' walk apart, and both were time travel in their own way.

### Ghosts of Summer at the Nebuta Museum WA RASSE

Right beside the train station, the **Nebuta Museum** makes a bold architectural statement out of red steel ribs meant to evoke the region's beech forests. Step inside and everything turns dark and hushed. Then you see them: four of the colossal, intricately crafted Nebuta floats that had danced through the streets during the famous summer festival just weeks earlier. Lit from within inside the darkened hall, they feel like sleeping giants. You can admire every obsessive detail—fierce warrior faces, coiling dragons, all built from wire, wood, and paper.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanSommer2025-Aomori/PXL_20250825_094131140.jpg" alt="" width="1000" height="1000" >}}

It's an oddly melancholic space. You feel the raw energy radiating off these floats, yet you encounter them outside the festival itself—without the thunderous drums, the dancers, and millions of spectators. It feels like meeting the ghost of the festival. To bridge that gap, they run a "Haneto Experience" three times a day where visitors can jump in and dance to the traditional music themselves. I watched the spectacle from a safe distance (while eating, naturally), as braver souls fearlessly hurled themselves into the dancing crowd. My own dance moves remained a closely guarded state secret that day. A standout touch was the "Hands-on Nebuta" corner, where you can actually touch a section of a real float—feeling the rough paper texture and sturdy wire frame gives you a real sense of how much craft goes into these fleeting works of art. Also not to be overlooked: the excellent tuna curry at the museum restaurant—an unexpected but very welcome recharge.

### A Ferry Full of Trains
Just a stone's throw away floats the *Hakkōda Maru*, a bright yellow relic from another era. Until the Seikan Tunnel opened in 1988, this ferry served as one of the main lifelines linking Japan's main island of Honshu to Hokkaido. Today it's a museum ship, and wandering its decks feels like stepping straight back into the Shōwa era. You can stroll through old passenger cabins, stand on the bridge, and picture the captain piloting the vessel across the Tsugaru Strait.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanSommer2025-Aomori/PXL_20250826_051611241.jpg" alt="" width="1000" height="1000" >}}
{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanSommer2025-Aomori/PXL_20250826_052345968.jpg" alt="" width="1000" height="1000" >}}

Yet the wildest, weirdest, most fascinating part of the ship hides down in its belly. You head down a staircase and walk out onto the vast vehicle deck—and sitting right there are actual train cars. An old express coach, a mail car, even a diesel locomotive. It's a surreal sight: trains inside a ship. The Hakkōda Maru was a rail ferry capable of hauling up to 48 railcars across four onboard tracks. That feat of engineering is obsolete now, a monument to an age overtaken by progress. Just like the Nebuta Museum, the Hakkōda Maru preserves something whose original purpose no longer exists. Both tell stories of Aomori's past—of ephemeral festivals and retired technology—and both leave a deep, thoughtful impression.

## Shrine Hopping
One day I decided to embark on a little spiritual quest and visit four shrines in one go. That tour quickly evolved from a polished tourist attraction into the mysterious blank spots on Aomori's map.

Stop number one was Hirota Shrine—what you might call the "beginner-friendly" shrine. It's unique, reputedly the only shrine in Japan historically dedicated to warding off illness and misfortune. It's also genuinely fun: they have *omikuji* (paper fortunes) shaped like apples and scallops, the symbols of the region. I loved the *mizu-mikuji* in particular—you draw a blank slip and float it in water, and your fortune materializes as if by magic. Cute crowdfunded goldfish Nebuta lanterns dangle overhead, creating an absurdly photogenic vibe. Hirota Shrine knows how to win visitors over.

After that, things got... weird. My itinerary led me to Shōichii Kinsei-inaka Shrine, Okidate Inari Shrine, and Kusushi Shrine. And that's where the trail of information ran dry. No glossy brochures, no English signs, no apple fortunes. Just silence. I stood before tiny, modest shrines hemmed in by residential houses, feeling oddly like a trespasser. Were these public spaces? Or had I accidentally wandered into Mr. Tanaka's private backyard shrine? I paced around trying to decode the stone lanterns and quiet halls, but they refused to give up their secrets.

## Afternoon Lighthouse
On the afternoon of August 27—around 14:10, to be exact—I walked out to the Aomori Port North Breakwater West Lighthouse. Unlike the playful Bay Bridge glowing in shifting colors after dark that I'd cross later, the lighthouse was its complete opposite: stoic, functional, timeless. It isn't there to look pretty; it's an active working beacon guiding ships safely into port. Standing out at the tip of the breakwater was a quiet moment of calm.

## An Evening in Light Rain
That evening on August 27, a gentle rain started falling. Not a storm—just a soft, cool drizzle that turned city lights into an impressionist painting. It made for the ideal evening to walk across the Aomori Bay Bridge. The bridge looks impressive enough by day, and at night it's usually a spectacle, with pylons deliberately shaped like the letter "A" for Aomori lit up in slowly shifting colors—except tonight, sadly, the lights were turned off. Still, the rain gave the asphalt a mirror sheen, reflecting every stray streetlamp into a near-futuristic, *Blade Runner*-esque mood. I strolled along the wide pedestrian walkway locals affectionately call the "Aomori Love Bridge," gazing out over the harbor where the Hakkōda Maru sat quietly at anchor—taking in the shimmer of lights around me and the dark bay stretching below.
