*Onsen Thoughts: Short, unfiltered notes and everyday observations—the kind of things that drift through your mind after a long day of walking, when you sink into hot spring water, steam rises around you, and the carousel of thoughts finally slows down.*

---

Anyone who rides trains in Japan knows the ubiquitous ritual: right on the platform, or at the latest upon stepping into the train car, the backpack almost reflexively migrates from the back to the front of the chest. Signs at stations and pictograms above the seats tirelessly remind passengers to be considerate and avoid whacking fellow commuters with bulky luggage strapped to their backs in tight crowds. A deeply Japanese gesture of lived politeness—or so you'd think.

<!--more-->

Yet as logical as this rule seems at first glance, it starts to crack under closer scrutiny. When you're wedged into the packed rush-hour crush between Shinjuku and Shibuya, it undeniably serves a purpose: you keep an eye on your bag, and nobody accidentally takes a backpack corner to the face when you turn around.

As soon as motion enters the equation, however, the principle flips into its opposite. People in public spaces aren't stationary pillars; they move—boarding, pushing toward the exit, hurrying across transfer stations. A fully loaded backpack strapped across your torso reliably blocks your downward line of sight. You can see neither your own feet nor stair steps or tripping hazards on the ground.

The inevitable result: steps become tentative, your stride turns awkward, and walking speed drops noticeably. Instead of keeping passenger flow smooth, front-wearing turns strolling and commuting passengers into artificial bottlenecks who suddenly stop or hesitate right in the middle of a dense human stream because they've lost sight of the floor.

This is where it's worth looking at a principle that Japanese culture otherwise masters to perfection: **Kaizen (改善)**—the continuous, incremental process of improvement in which even time-honored routines are put to the test without prejudice.

In the spirit of Kaizen, wouldn't adapting the habit be a far better solution? While in motion—walking across platforms and through station corridors—the backpack simply stays on your back: your field of vision stays clear, your posture stays natural, and your pace remains fluid. Then, once you're standing in a packed train car, it simply moves down into your hand or between your feet. Nobody gets crowded at head or chest height, your center of gravity drops lower, and your forward view remains completely unobstructed.

Does the benefit of taking up slightly less space really outweigh that drawback?
