In travel guides, Hakodate is mostly famous for its spectacular night view and well-preserved historic buildings from the era when the port first opened to the world. For me personally, though, the city at the southern tip of Hokkaido was also an absolute highlight for a very pragmatic, logistical reason: you can explore it wonderfully and efficiently without setting foot on a single bus.

Anyone who knows me and my previous trip reports knows that I'm not exactly a fan of buses—whether it's the often confusing timetables or the cramped ride. Which made it all the more refreshing that here, the historic streetcar ("Shiden") isn't just a tourist gimmick, but the actual backbone of local public transit. The clatter of the wheels on the rails, the solid mechanics of the sometimes very old cars, and the clear route layout turn getting around into pure joy. You hop on, enjoy the ride through the port city, and arrive relaxed at almost every spot that matters.

# Fort Goryōkaku

Getting to this impressive site is a breeze on said tram. Even as you step off and take the short walk into the park, you immediately feel the historical weight and sheer scale of the place. It is Japan's first Western-style fort, laid out in a perfect five-pointed star. For anyone who appreciates symmetry and well-thought-out engineering, this place is fascinating.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/Hakodate2025/PXL_20251218_004947068.jpg" alt="The tram to Goryōkaku" width="1400" height="1400" >}}

From the neighboring 107-meter-tall Goryōkaku Tower you get the famous "postcard view" from above showing off the star shape in all its glory, yet I enjoyed exploring the geometry on foot almost even more. Only down at ground level do you really grasp the tactical layout of the ramparts and moats. You stroll along the fortifications surrounded by hundreds of cherry trees, which (depending on the season) soften the fort's strict military lines and radiate an almost meditative calm.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/Hakodate2025/PXL_20251218_025706073.jpg" alt="Postcard view" width="1400" height="1400" >}}


That Japan never loses sight of the essentials—or the quirky details—even in historic places became obvious to me in a rather unexpected spot. Even the restroom facilities here are apparently designed to blend harmoniously (or at least photogenically) into the overall scenery. Definitely worth a snapshot.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/Hakodate2025/PXL_20251218_013741693.jpg" alt="Restroom at the fort" width="1400" height="1400" >}}

# Motomachi and the Slopes

Another area that's fantastic to explore on foot is the Motomachi district at the foot of Mount Hakodate. This neighborhood is famous for its topography. Its signature feature are the steep, arrow-straight streets ("zaka") running straight down from the mountain to the harbor basin. The climb is quite the workout and easily replaces leg day at the gym, but every meter of elevation rewards you with even better views of the bay and the ships anchored below.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/Hakodate2025/PXL_20251219_043204494.PANO-EDIT.jpg" alt="Walking the steep streets of Motomachi" width="1400" height="1400" >}}

Here you'll find an architecturally fascinating mash-up that reflects Hakodate's history as one of Japan's first open trading ports: Russian Orthodox churches with their onion domes stand right next to old Japanese buildings, British consulates, and Chinese assembly halls. It's a walk across cultures, stitched together by steep cobblestones.

# Mount Hakodate

Of course, no trip to Hakodate is complete without its classic lookout point. Instead of squeezing into a packed, swaying tour bus to suffer up the switchbacks, though, a far more technically elegant ropeway carries you straight to the summit. The ride itself already gives you a taste of the panorama to come.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/Hakodate2025/PXL_20251218_080605928.jpg" alt="Route to Mt. Hakodate" width="1400" height="1400" >}}
{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/Hakodate2025/PXL_20251218_080233824.PANO.jpg" alt="Observation deck" width="1400" height="1400" >}}

Once you reach the observation deck at the top, you quickly understand why this view earned three stars in the Michelin Green Guide and is frequently named one of the three best night views in the world. The city sits on a narrow, hourglass-shaped spit of land framed on both sides by dark sea. When the city lights flicker on, it's a sight you won't soon forget—a perfect contrast between the black ocean and glowing urban life.

Yet even the finest view and the purest analog experience aren't safe from digital reality these days. Right in the middle of soaking in the moment, just as I wanted to capture the perfect lighting, my Google Pixel 9 Pro XL decided that this exact instant was the ideal time for an unpostponable forced update.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/Hakodate2025/Screenshot_20251218-170638.png" alt="Pixel update screen" width="1400" height="1400" >}}

Timing really is everything. While the software updated and the progress bar crept slowly along, at least I was able (read: forced) to focus entirely on the city's "hardware." Sometimes technology just forces you into offline bliss.

Food-wise, by the way, Hakodate was an absolute win—which isn't always a given in Japan if there are certain textures you'd rather avoid. Fresh fish and seafood—especially the famous squid (*ika*)—are everywhere here. And thankfully there was no rice pudding in sight anywhere, a dish I famously hold in about the same esteem as bus rides. Instead, there are local institutions like "Carl Raymon."

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/Hakodate2025/PXL_20251219_022003987.jpg" alt="100th Anniversary Carl Raymon" width="1400" height="1400" >}}
{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/Hakodate2025/PXL_20251219_022504390.jpg" alt="100th Anniversary Carl Raymon" width="1400" height="1400" >}}

Who's actually behind that name? Carl Raymon (born Karl Weidel Raymon) was a master butcher from what was then Bohemia, whose journeyman years brought him all the way to Hakodate. He is regarded as the father of authentic sausage and ham making in Hokkaido. Despite tough times during the war years, he stayed in the country—for love of his Japanese wife—and perfected his craft. Biting into a snappy sausage made according to German purity traditions on the opposite side of the globe is a surreal, yet wonderful moment.
