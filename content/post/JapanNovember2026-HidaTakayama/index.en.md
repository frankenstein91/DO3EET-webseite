After yesterday's sunny day in Gifu, it was time to pack up in the morning and check out of the Dormy Inn. From **JR Gifu** station, I boarded the **Limited Express Hida 503** heading north into the mountains toward **Takayama**. The day turned out to pack plenty of highlights: live hybrid powertrain displays and mistimed tunnel announcements on the train, the open-air village **Hida no Sato** in the rain, a long walk back through Takayama to **Hie Shrine** at dusk, a surprising **Suzuki Cappuccino** *itasha*, and a steaming street footbath to bring tired feet back to life.

## Hybrid Tech Live Above the Car Door on Hida 503

The Hida is no ordinary diesel express, but a modern **hybrid train** (JR Central's HC85 series) — and anyone with a soft spot for engineering gets to watch the powertrain work in real time. Mounted above the sliding door in **Car 8**, a wide display doesn't just show the car number and *Reserved* status, but also animates the live energy flow between **Engine**, **Battery**, and **Motor**.

During steady cruising, blue-and-yellow arrows flow straight from the engine's generator to the traction motor (`Engine → Motor`), while the battery sits idle in between.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-HidaTakayama/PXL_20261117_001819469.jpg" alt="Hybrid display inside Limited Express Hida Car 8 showing energy flowing from Engine to Motor" width="1200" height="675" >}}

Just moments later, the battery visibly joins in: **“⚡ Battery Assisting”** lights up on the right side of the screen, and animated arrows stream simultaneously from both **Engine** and **Battery** into the **Motor**. It is the kind of onboard display that makes you glance above the door almost as often as out the window.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-HidaTakayama/PXL_20261117_001835172.jpg" alt="Interior of Hida Car 8 with the overhead display showing 'Battery Assisting' as both Engine and Battery power the Motor" width="1200" height="675" >}}

## A Fish Train at Mino-Ōta & Scenic Announcements vs. Tunnel Timing

Along the way, during the stop at **Mino-Ōta Station (美濃太田 / みのおおた)**, an eye-catching sight sat across on the opposite platform: a brightly wrapped **“fish train”** operated by the **Nagaragawa Railway**.

Titled **“Seiryū Nagaragawa no Ayu” (清流長良川の鮎)**, the railcar is covered end to end in illustrations of river fish from the clear waters of the Nagara River — especially *ayu* (sweetfish) — alongside anglers, cormorants, and riverside scenes. Against an otherwise quiet, overcast platform, this rolling freshwater mural stood out instantly.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-HidaTakayama/PXL_20261117_002835421.jpg" alt="Colorfully wrapped 'Seiryū Nagaragawa no Ayu' fish train of the Nagaragawa Railway at Mino-Ōta Station" width="1200" height="675" >}}

As the line winds deeper into the mountains, the scenery outside becomes increasingly dramatic. To match, the Hida plays recorded sightseeing announcements pointing out notable views along the route in both **Japanese and English**.

In practice, though, the bilingual timing comes with a bit of unintentional comedy: while the Japanese announcement lands right before the scenic stretch, the **English translation sometimes arrives just a beat too late**. Just as the English voice finishes telling passengers which side to look out of, the train dives straight into the **next tunnel** — leaving everyone staring at dark concrete walls instead of the view.

## Arrival in Takayama & Rainy Hida no Sato Open-Air Village

Upon arriving in **Takayama**, the first quick stop was dropping off the luggage before heading out into the drizzle by **bus** to the **Hida no Sato (飛騨の里 / Hida Folk Village)** open-air museum. As it turned out, that single bus ride would be the only motorized leg in town — everything for the rest of the day I explored on foot.

Even from the very first glance across the pond, the village feels wonderfully timeless: historic farmhouses with steep, moss-covered **Gasshō-zukuri** thatched roofs reflect in the calm water, framed by orange, gold, and crimson autumn leaves beneath the overcast rain sky.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-HidaTakayama/PXL_20261117_031231439.jpg" alt="View across the pond at Hida no Sato showing moss-covered Gasshō-zukuri farmhouses surrounded by autumn foliage" width="1200" height="675" >}}

As an information board in the village explains, Hida no Sato owes its existence not just to heritage preservation, but also to the rapid changes of the late 1950s: when huge hydroelectric dams were built across the valleys of the Hida region and entire villages faced submersion, characteristic historic farmhouses were rescued, dismantled, and faithfully rebuilt here on higher ground.

Along the winding paths, small historical details are everywhere — from an old wooden fire-pump shed housing two vintage hand-pumped fire carts and coiled hoses, to moss-carpeted thatched roofs glistening quietly in the rain.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-HidaTakayama/PXL_20261117_031739540.jpg" alt="Inside the historic wooden fire-pump shed at Hida no Sato with two vintage hand-pumped fire carts" width="1200" height="675" >}}

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-HidaTakayama/PXL_20261117_033558753.jpg" alt="Steep moss-covered thatched roof of a Gasshō-zukuri farmhouse in the rain at Hida no Sato" width="1200" height="675" >}}

Tucked slightly off the main path among tall conifers and a thick carpet of fallen leaves sits a wonderfully rustic structure: a simple **woodcutter's hut (*Somagoya / 杣小屋*)** built from rough vertical timber planks, poles, and a bark roof weighted down with stones.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-HidaTakayama/PXL_20261117_035228010.jpg" alt="Rustic historic woodcutter's hut Somagoya among autumn leaves in the woods of Hida no Sato" width="1200" height="675" >}}

## Bell Resonance in the Rain, Rope-Bound Rafters & Ninja Taiyaki

Cutting gently through the rainy quiet of the open-air village came a deep, warm tone: the **bell** at Hida no Sato. With soft rain pattering in the background, its rich resonance seemed to carry across half the valley — a moment I captured as a short audio clip.

{{< audio src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-HidaTakayama/HidaNoSato_Glocke.m4a" title="Sound of the bell at Hida no Sato (with rain in the background)" >}}

Further along the trail, a moss-covered, weathered stone guardian figure watches beside stone steps before another grand farmhouse rises behind fiery red Japanese maple (*momiji*).

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-HidaTakayama/PXL_20261117_042303611.jpg" alt="Weathered moss-covered stone guardian statue beside stone steps at Hida no Sato" width="1200" height="675" >}}

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-HidaTakayama/PXL_20261117_042828153.jpg" alt="Historic Gasshō-zukuri farmhouse at Hida no Sato framed by bright red and orange autumn maple leaves" width="1200" height="675" >}}

Stepping inside the farmhouses is just as rewarding: in one room, traditional black lacquerware bowls, trays, and porcelain sake flasks are laid out on bright red felt for festive occasions. Looking upward into the steep attic levels reveals an even greater feat of craftsmanship: the massive timber rafters are assembled entirely without iron nails, lashed together with thick, tightly knotted **straw ropes** and preserved dark by decades of hearth smoke.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-HidaTakayama/PXL_20261117_043133262.jpg" alt="Traditional black lacquerware dining sets and sake bottles arranged on red felt inside a farmhouse" width="1200" height="675" >}}

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-HidaTakayama/PXL_20261117_043331061.jpg" alt="Looking up at the steep thatched roof rafters bound together with straw ropes inside a Gasshō-zukuri house" width="1200" height="675" >}}

In the chilly rain, a quick warm-up break indoors hit the spot — with a hot vending-machine can of **Suntory BOSS “Torokeru Cocoa” (とろけるココア)** resting on a heavy wooden stump (those Japanese vending-machine cans feel practically as sturdy and indestructible as old Nokia phones) — before snapping one last lakeside view of the tiny, thick-mossed **watermill shed** perched on stones at the edge of the pond.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-HidaTakayama/PXL_20261117_051453845.jpg" alt="Hot can of Suntory BOSS Torokeru Cocoa resting on a weathered wooden stump inside Hida no Sato" width="600" height="1067" >}}

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-HidaTakayama/PXL_20261117_060214497.jpg" alt="Small moss-roofed watermill shed on stone foundations by the pond at Hida no Sato" width="600" height="1067" >}}

And as a sweet finale before leaving the village, I treated myself to a fun twist on **taiyaki**: instead of the usual sea bream mold, this one came shaped as a **Ninja Taiyaki** — complete with shuriken, sword, and headband — piping hot and filled with rich **custard cream**.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-HidaTakayama/PXL_20261117_061602416.jpg" alt="Warm Ninja-themed taiyaki pastry molded with a sword and shuriken on brown paper" width="1200" height="675" >}}

## On Foot Through Takayama: Denver Park, Genkō-ji & Hie Shrine at Dusk

From Hida no Sato onward, I covered the entire return and town loop **on foot**. Along the way sits an unexpected nod to the American West right in the Japanese Alps: **City of Denver Park**, celebrating Takayama's long-standing sister city of Denver, Colorado. Not far beyond stands the imposing, weathered wooden gate of **Genkō-ji (全祝山 玄興寺)** with its intricate bracket carvings and glowing signboard tucked under the eaves.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-HidaTakayama/PXL_20261117_064443534.jpg" alt="City of Denver Park sign surrounded by autumn leaves in Takayama" width="1200" height="675" >}}

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-HidaTakayama/PXL_20261117_065755442.jpg" alt="Weathered wooden temple gate of Genkō-ji in Takayama" width="1200" height="675" >}}

My main late-afternoon destination lay at the southeastern edge of town beneath tall cedars: historic **Hie Shrine (山王宮 日枝神社 / Sannō-gū)**. Right by the lower stone steps, a vivid Japanese maple blazed in shades of scarlet and tangerine above the rain-darkened stone, while a nearby stone lantern wore a thick cap of fallen maple leaves.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-HidaTakayama/PXL_20261117_072114362.jpg" alt="Vivid red and orange Japanese maple beside the stone steps and lanterns of Hie Shrine in Takayama" width="1200" height="675" >}}

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-HidaTakayama/PXL_20261117_072611956.jpg" alt="Stone lantern at Hie Shrine topped and surrounded by fallen red maple leaves" width="600" height="1067" >}}

As **dusk** settled in beneath the tall cedars, patience was needed at first — the lights did not switch on until around **4:52 p.m.** Turning them on a little earlier, closer to **4:30 p.m.**, would have suited the twilight atmosphere much better, and honestly I also wished that more of the lamps across the shrine grounds had lit up.

Still, the spots that did illuminate made up for it: the **hanachōzu** purification basin, packed with colorful chrysanthemums, glowed beneath two wooden lanterns against the darkening forest. Just beside it, the tall vermilion **torii** lit up at the foot of the stone stairway, while red-trimmed wooden lanterns (with a stone fox peeking out behind) and glowing maple leaves under the eaves still created a wonderfully atmospheric evening scene.

## Evening Finale: Suzuki Cappuccino Itasha & Warming Up in a Street Footbath

Walking back across the rain-slicked evening streets of Takayama, a great surprise for car enthusiasts rolled right past me: a tiny red **Suzuki Cappuccino** (one of the beloved 1990s kei roadsters) sitting on humble steel wheels and wrapped as an **itasha**, complete with an anime character on the door and a “Love and Peace” graphic across the trunk lid (license plates blurred for privacy, of course).

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-HidaTakayama/PXL_20261117_082622544.jpg" alt="Red Suzuki Cappuccino kei roadster in itasha livery on a wet evening street in Takayama with license plates blurred" width="1200" height="675" >}}

After walking all those kilometers through the damp November chill, there could hardly have been a better ending to the day in Takayama than a public **street footbath (*ashiyu / 足湯*)**. Shoes and socks off, feet straight into the wonderfully hot mineral water, while a steady stream poured endlessly from the wooden trough — pure bliss for tired feet after a packed day stretching from hybrid train tech and thatched mountain roofs to glowing shrine lanterns.
