Christmas in Japan is famously a cultural curiosity—more a holiday for couples strolling through tunnels of fairy lights and families gathering around buckets of fried chicken than a season of quiet contemplation or religious devotion. Yet anyone looking to escape that commercial hustle—or at least trade it for an authentic backdrop of waist-deep snow and tangible history—inevitably heads north to Hokkaido.

From December 24 to 26, 2025, I spent the holidays in the nostalgic port town of Otaru before catching the train onward to the vibrant metropolis of Sapporo. It wasn't merely a sightseeing tour, but a journey of stark contrasts: between spiritual calm in snowy (damp) shrines and a nearly physical battle amid the tourist chaos around a steam clock; between views earned the hard way in biting cold and moments of quiet reverence before the island's pioneer history.

# Otaru: Harbor Romance and the Fight for the Time of Day

My arrival in Otaru on Christmas Eve was greeted by the expected cold and the harbor's rough, unvarnished beauty. The Otaru Canal and port area—once the bustling heart of the herring trade—take on a character all their own in winter. The old stone warehouses reflected in the dark water tell of an era when money flowed here as freely as snow piled onto the rooftops. Wander away from the main drag, with the sea breeze blowing in and driving ice crystals into your face, and you feel the city's history far more vividly and honestly than inside the polished, warmly lit souvenir shops of Sakaimachi Street, where everything revolves around cheesecake and glassware.

Naturally, visiting the famous Otaru Steam Clock is mandatory on any visitor's itinerary. Yet right here, the ugly face of global overtourism reared its head—proof that not even remote Hokkaido is spared. The clock itself, a gift from Otaru's Canadian sister city Vancouver, is a fascinating piece of engineering that whistles out puffs of steam and plays a tune every xx minutes. Sadly, the magic of its Victorian charm was drowned out in a reckless crush of people.

Hordes of Asian tourists, to whom the concepts of courtesy, queuing, or personal space appeared utterly alien, turned watching the steam whistles into a test of patience rather than a pleasure. People shoved, selfie sticks nearly poked into my nose, and the moment the clock played its melody was swallowed up by loud chatter and shouting. It was the kind of moment that makes you wonder whether you're part of the problem or just an innocent bystander in an absurd stage play.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/OtaruUndSapporo/PXL_20251225_025904160_exported_37800_ng.jpg" alt="The Otaru Steam Clock under siege" width="1000" height="1000" >}}

My shrine visits offered a healing, almost therapeutic contrast to all that. Suitengu Shrine, perched majestically atop a steep hill, offered not only spiritual stillness but also an unobstructed, sweeping view over the harbor—far removed from the noise and sweet aromas of the shopping street. Up here, the snow lay untouched, the air was crisp, and the only sound was the crunch under my own boots.
Before finally boarding the train to Sapporo on December 26, I paid one last visit to Sumiyoshi Shrine. It was the ideal farewell to Otaru: the silence of the shrine, its red torii glowing almost unreal against the deep white snow, wiped away the stress of the crowds. It was a mental "reset" that cleared my head and prepared me for the rush of the big city.

# Sapporo: Aiming High (and Looking Way Down)

The train ride from Otaru to Sapporo is short—barely 40 minutes—yet it marks a dramatic shift from a historic, almost village-like port town to the modern, grid-planned metropolis of the north. Parts of the route hug the coastline so closely that the waves nearly lap at the tracks—a spectacular panorama. Once in Sapporo, I headed up high—more than once—to take in the city from a bird's-eye view.

The Sapporo TV Tower at the end of Odori Park serves up the classic vista along the city's central axis. Especially in winter, when the park sits beneath a thick blanket of snow and the "White Illumination" turns the trees into a sea of lights, the view is kitschy in the best possible way. Yet the true icon among Sapporo's observation decks—the one place you simply have to see—is the JR Tower.

The view from the 38th floor ("T38") is spectacular, no doubt about it—you can see out to the mountains and across the endless sprawl of the urban grid. But let's be honest: for male visitors, the real highlight—spoken of only in hushed tones—is arguably Japan's most famous urinal. The architects deliberately placed it right up against the glass facade. There is something surreally liberating, almost godlike, about answering nature's call while staring straight through floor-to-ceiling windows at the sprawling city of millions below, looking like an anthill beneath your feet. Here, design meets... well, relief. It is a quiet dialogue between man and metropolis—intimate and public all at once.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/OtaruUndSapporo/PXL_20251227_091816309.jpg" alt="The famous panoramic urinal at JR Tower" width="1000" height="1000" >}}

I had less luck initially with my trip up the Mount Moiwa Ropeway. My plan was textbook romantic: ride to the top, then soak in the view. Reality served up an impenetrable gray wall of fog and blowing snow. Neither sun nor stars—just dismal visibility and an overcast sky swallowing the city whole. I stood there freezing and asked myself why I had paid for this. Patience pays off, though: later it cleared up just enough for the wind to tear gaps in the cloud cover, giving the mountain a chance to reveal a moody, partial view over snow-white Sapporo. It wasn't the picture-postcard shot you hope for, yet perhaps for that exact reason a more honest memory of the unpredictable northern winter.

# Diving into Hokkaido's History

Beyond observation towers and temples of consumerism, I also dedicated time to the island's profound and often brutal history. A visit to the Hokkaido Museum and the Historical Village of Hokkaido (Kaitaku-no Mura) is practically mandatory if you want to understand how the wild, unforgiving northern frontier became modern-day Hokkaido.

The Historical Village—an open-air museum featuring more than 50 relocated historic buildings—is especially striking in winter. The old wooden structures, from tiny police boxes to grand mansions, stand in the deep snow like ghosts from a bygone era. Walking along the snow-packed lanes past horse-drawn sleighs still making their rounds, you literally stroll back through time. You start shivering despite a modern down jacket and get a real sense of what the pioneers must have endured.

From the old train station to the spartan settlers' cabins—it is deeply humbling to see the extreme conditions people once lived under here, without insulated glazing or central heating. The contrast with the creature comforts of modern travel—heated Shinkansen trains, overheated hotel rooms, and high-tech observation decks—couldn't be sharper. It teaches humility before nature and respect for the generations who tamed this land.
