After the layover in Frankfurt and waiting at the gate, it was finally time to board NH204 to Tokyo Haneda. A long-haul flight on All Nippon Airways promises its usual high comfort—yet sometimes throws in completely unexpected tech quirks and mini-adventures before your feet even touch Japanese soil.

From digital censorship on the moving map and an involuntary refresher on final approach to a first full day split between shimmering goldfish, spectacular views, and the quirks of modern camera sensors: kicking off the trip in Japan certainly didn't lack for material.

## Oddities at 33,000 Feet: Redacted Map Bars Over China and a Menu Truce

Let's start the trip with two observations from inside the Dreamliner cabin.

### Geofencing on the Moving-Map Display

During a long night flight across the Asian continent, it's always worth keeping an eye on the inflight entertainment system's interactive moving map if you're into tech and geography. Over northern China, near coordinates **39°00' N, 114°00' E** (heading 105.0°), a remarkable detail caught my eye:

While historical and regional place names like *Kalgan* (today's Zhangjiakou in Hebei Province) appeared completely normally on the virtual globe, just a few centimeters to the southeast—right where Beijing sits—loomed a solid, opaque **black censor bar**.

To avoid any copyright trouble over the system vendor's map artwork, I'm skipping the photo of the screen here. The phenomenon itself, though, is a fascinating case study in geofencing, regulatory requirements, and software compliance in civil aviation: to secure overflight permits and satisfy country-specific rules, avionics and IFE vendors (such as Collins Aerospace, Panasonic Avionics, or Thales) sometimes have to dynamically black out or hide sensitive regions, capitals, or military zones on their moving maps the moment an aircraft enters the relevant airspace.

### A Culinary Peace Treaty at Cruising Altitude

Welcome side effect of the flight: the "codeshare dilemma" around special meals I wrote about earlier resolved itself in the nicest way possible.

For dinner, the crew served an excellent **Japanese beef curry** with steamed rice. And the real highlight arrived at breakfast shortly before touchdown: as the Western option, they actually served the **seafood & gnocchi in cream sauce** I'd spotted on the menu! Even with a broken pre-order form beforehand, the universe (or ANA's head chef) smiled down on me—a fitting finale to the inflight dining.

## Final Approach to Haneda: Free Shower From Above

Shortly before landing at Tokyo Haneda Airport (HND), the end of the long-haul flight drew near. The seatbelt signs chimed on, the announcement rang out: *"Cabin crew, prepare for landing."* Both passengers and cabin crew were strapped firmly into their seats.

At that exact moment, I suddenly felt stray, ice-cold drops hitting my head and shoulders: from the seam between the ceiling panels above the overhead bins, water was steadily finding its way downward.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag1/PXL_20261108_231744080.jpg" alt="Water droplets on the aircraft ceiling during final approach" width="600" height="1067" >}}

Complaining or flagging someone down was out of the question at that point since the flight attendants were already buckled into their jumpseats and nobody was allowed up. So the only option was to sit tight, grab a tissue, and laugh it off.

Technically speaking, this is a well-known aviation phenomenon aptly dubbed **"Cabin Rain"** or "Rain in the Plane." At cruising altitude, the aircraft's outer skin gets bitterly cold. Humid air exhaled by passengers rises into the gap between the cabin ceiling panels and the insulation, often freezing into frost or a layer of ice. Once the plane starts descending into warmer air and cabin pressure shifts slightly, that ice melts all at once and the meltwater drips straight through the panel seams into the cabin.

## Arriving in Tokyo: Bag Drop in Toyosu and Goldfish in Ginza

After touchdown and breezing through immigration at Haneda, the first reach went straight for the PASMO card. Top up 10,000 yen, grab a cold drink from a vending machine, and into the city:

The **Tokyo Monorail** carried us to the terminus at *Hamamatsuchō*, and from there via *Takeshiba* on the fully automated **Yurikamome** elevated transit line out across Tokyo Bay to *Shijō-Mae* (Toyosu). Since it was still way too early in the morning for regular hotel check-in, we dropped the heavy suitcases at the front desk first so we could hit the city light-footed and clear-headed.

Continuing on the Yurikamome and Tokyo Metro Yūrakuchō Line took us straight into upscale **Ginza**. Our destination there was the [ART AQUARIUM Museum](http://artaquarium.jp/) inside the Ginza Mitsukoshi department store. The museum showcases traditional Japanese ornamental goldfish (*kingyo*) inside elaborately sculpted glass vessels paired with modern lighting, soundscapes, and shifting color worlds—a mesmerizing visual clash between centuries-old breeding tradition and contemporary Japanese installation art.

## Roppongi Hills & Tokyo City View: When Dusk Makes Up for Everything

After wandering through the streets of Ginza and Toranomon, we headed onward to **Roppongi Hills**—specifically the *Mori Tower*.

Up top, to be honest, we were skeptical at first: the daytime weather was overcast and hazy gray, the tiered ticket pricing gave us brief pause, and to top it off, the observation decks weren't fully open (the outdoor Sky Deck was closed, among other things).

We went ahead with **Tokyo City View** anyway—and waiting it out paid off handsomely. As dusk set in, the dull gray transformed into a dramatic backdrop:

## Tokyo Mega Illumination: A Sea of Lights and the Limits of Camera Sensors

After heading back down and taking a short walk to *Azabu-Jūban* Station, we rode the Toei Ōedo Line back to *Daimon/Hamamatsuchō* and hopped south on the Tokyo Monorail to **Ōi Keibajō Mae**.

During the winter months, the sprawling grounds of Oi Racecourse (*Tokyo City Keiba*) host **Tokyo Mega Illumination**—a massive light installation with spectacular light tunnels, illuminated fields, and water shows right across the racetrack infield.

To the naked eye on site, the whole thing was downright overwhelming—yet from a photography standpoint, it delivered an intriguing realization:

Modern smartphone cameras (such as the Pixel 9 Pro XL I was using), with their high-resolution sensors and aggressive computational HDR algorithms, are tuned to pull out micro-contrast and render point light sources tack-sharp. At a light display like this, that exact strength backfires: instead of capturing the soft, blending, shimmering "sea of light" your eyes perceive, the camera resolves every single tiny LED diode individually and clinically. As a result, the image loses some of its magic and looks flatter and more technical than reality felt in person. A fascinating tug-of-war between physics, optics, and computational photography.

## Obstacle Course Back: Scavenger Hunt at Tennōzu Isle

After looping around the huge racecourse grounds, we set off back toward the hotel later that evening—which still had one small logistical curveball in store.

From *Ōi Keibajō Mae*, we took the Tokyo Monorail to **Tennōzu Isle**, where we needed to transfer to the underground **Rinkai Line**. Anyone familiar with Tokyo's transit map knows: not every transfer on paper means walking across a platform. Here at Tennōzu Isle, the elevated monorail and the Rinkai Line sit inside two completely separate structures.

You tap out through the monorail fare gates and have to navigate your way across pedestrian bridges, streets, and office complexes. In the dark of night on tired legs, the entrance to the Rinkai Line proved surprisingly elusive—a proper scavenger hunt for tiny logos and signs before finally riding the escalators deep underground!

From there, it was back to smooth sailing: Rinkai Line to *Kokusai-Tenjijō*, a short walk over to the Yurikamome station *Ariake*, and one last brief hop back to *Shijō-Mae*, where a well-earned hotel check-in and a comfy bed finally awaited after a marathon travel day.
