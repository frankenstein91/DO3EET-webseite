Astrophotography from home sometimes calls for creative solutions. Although I have a garden and yard available, setting up valuable equipment outside unattended for several hours overnight always carries a risk of theft. Observing directly through a roof window is significantly safer in that regard.

To position the Seestar S30 Pro stably, securely, and gently at the roof window, I built a custom mounting rig—a "table" without a tabletop:

* **Material & Profile**: The frame is constructed from 20 × 20 mm aluminum extrusion profiles (Slot 6 / Nut 6). *(Note: Unfortunately, no photograph of this rig is currently available.)*
* **Variable Leg Lengths**: The "table" features legs of different lengths to perfectly compensate for the geometry and pitch of the roof slope.
* **The Roof Window "Claw"**: Two 10 cm long legs function as a claw/hook mechanism, allowing the rig to latch securely onto the roof window frame and stay firmly in place.
* **DIY Frame Protection**: To protect the window frame from scratches caused by the aluminum profiles, each claw leg is carefully padded: wrapped first in an old cloth handkerchief and then covered with an old sock.
* **Telescope Mount**: An adapter translating from an M6 bolt to a standard 3/8-inch tripod thread is attached to one upper edge. The Seestar S30 Pro mounts solidly onto this 3/8" thread. *(A humorous shout-out goes to audio equipment manufacturers here: without stage and microphone gear producers, we probably wouldn't have such handy M6-to-camera-thread adapters!)*

Thanks to this DIY mount, the smart telescope can be operated conveniently, securely, and without damaging the window frame. It was with this exact setup during the night of July 25–26, 2026, that I captured an impressive mosaic of our neighboring galaxy, the **Andromeda Galaxy (Messier 31)**.

## Setup & Compass Reboot

As noted in my previous [solar observation review]({{< ref "post/ZWOSeestarS30ProSolar" >}}), the Seestar's internal compass can occasionally misalign on the very first slew of a session. Before targeting M31, I initially slewed to the Moon. Sure enough, the compass pointing was off on the first attempt.

A quick reboot of the telescope solved the issue immediately. Following the restart, celestial positioning was spot-on as usual. (You can check out the resulting Moon capture on [AstroImg](https://www.astroimg.com/thread/434531?shareUserId=627588482457407491&shareId=1785137243306)).

## Mosaic Framing in the Seestar App

Setting up the framing for the Andromeda Galaxy in the Seestar app was remarkably smooth. Dragging across the interactive star map, along with rotating and zooming within the Framing menu, makes framing large targets intuitive and accurate.

Because M31 spans over 3° across the sky, it is far too large for the telescope's native field of view. Using Mosaic Mode expanded the FOV to approximately **3.8° × 2.2°**, providing ample space to frame the entire galaxy along with its companion objects.

### Filter Selection: IRCUT vs. LP
I deliberately chose the internal **IRCUT filter** over the LP (Light Pollution / dual-band) filter. Galaxies are broadband continuum emitters that radiate across the entire optical spectrum; using a light pollution filter would cut out too much of the galaxy's natural starlight.

## Observing Conditions & Battery Performance

The conditions during this summer night were excellent:
* **Location & Sky**: Observed from a village location rated at **Bortle 4.7**. No clouds were present—neither visually to the naked eye nor on DWD satellite weather maps.
* **Temperature & Sensor**: Ambient temperature was **12 °C** (dew point **10 °C**). Due to high humidity, I enabled the internal dew heater for the final 30–40 minutes of the session. Sensor temperature fluctuated between **27.5 °C and 29 °C**.
* **Capture Timestamp**: The final stacked mosaic exposure was completed early in the morning on **July 26, 2026 at 03:23 AM (CEST)**.
* **Battery Endurance**: The entire session ran on internal battery power without an external power bank. Across nearly **81 minutes** of continuous exposure (485 × 10 s sub-frames) plus dew heating, the battery performed admirably, finishing with **25% remaining capacity**.


## Thermal Behavior at the Roof Window

It is often claimed that observing through an open roof window causes severe turbulence due to warm indoor air or heat radiating from roof tiles. In practice: **No negative impacts from thermal currents or airflow were noticeable.** The Seestar stacked frames without any trouble.

### Identified Celestial Objects:
* **M31 (NGC 224)**: Andromeda Galaxy with its bright nucleus and intricate dust lanes.
* **M32 (NGC 221)**: Elliptical dwarf galaxy (south of the M31 core).
* **M110 (NGC 205)**: Elliptical dwarf galaxy (northwest of M31).
* **NGC 206**: Massive star-forming region / H-II complex in M31's spiral arm.
* **Foreground Stars**: ν Andromedae (ν And, Mag ~4.5) as well as 32 & 39 Andromedae.

*Image note:* Near the top edge, two faint parallel lines are visible—likely caused by a satellite or aircraft passing through the field of view during the session.
