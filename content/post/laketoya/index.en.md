There are places in Japan that seem to sink into a deep, almost meditative slumber in winter. Lake Toya (Tōyako) is one of those places—at least at first glance. When I arrived here on December 20, a good two months after my time in Utsunomiya, Hokkaido welcomed me not with the gentle chill of autumn, but with the merciless, slick cold of the north. My plan was simple: arrive, explore the region's volcanic power on foot, and move on by December 22. Yet as so often happens when traveling, the weather usually writes the script itself.

<!--more-->

Lake Toya is actually famous for almost never freezing over—one of Japan's northernmost ice-free lakes, thanks to the geothermal activity bubbling beneath the surface. Still, what good is an open lake when the trails leading to it are one giant sheet of ice?

# The Subjunctive Mood of Hiking: Toyako to Toyoura

I had big plans. My Komoot route was mapped out and ready to go: a proper hike from Toyako to Toyoura. It wasn't supposed to be a casual lakeside stroll, but a close encounter with the Earth's destructive and creative forces.

The plan was to follow the Nishiyama Crater Walking Path (西山火口散策路) and continue on to the Konpira Crater Disaster Remains (金比羅火口災害遺構散策路). These aren't ancient ruins, but stark witnesses to the eruption of 2000, where the earth tore roads apart and swallowed houses whole. I wanted to see how nature is reclaiming the area.

Reality, however, had other ideas: sheer ice. Not just a dusting of frost, but that treacherous kind of glaze that turns every step into a roll of the dice. Safety comes first, especially when you're out on your own. The big craters would have to wait. Instead of the 6.5-kilometer route with 140 meters of elevation gain I had saved as a PDF on my phone, it turned into a study in slowing down.

# The Aesthetics of Cold and Lights in the Dark

When grand adventures fall through, your eye sharpens for the smaller details. Instead of elevation meters, I collected moments of stillness. The lake itself, with Nakajima Island sitting in the middle, feels almost surreal in winter. The air is crisp enough to sting your lungs, and the light has a clarity you won't find in summer.

It's an odd contrast: you know the ground beneath you is geothermally alive and hot springs feed the hotels (and yes, soaking in the onsen after those icy walks wasn't optional, it was survival), while up above, the wind whips snow across the asphalt.

Since the days are short—in December, the sun feels like it clocks out right after lunch—sightseeing shifted into the dark. Toyako fights off the winter gloom with lights. The Illumination Tunnel and illuminated streets give the town an almost fairy-tale vibe at night, even if it leans a little tacky. Still, when you're walking around in sub-zero temperatures, those lights at least warm the soul.

# World Politics in the Rearview Mirror

Between slick paths and the quiet lake, I quite literally stumbled into a piece of world history. In 2008, Toyako was the center of the political universe during the 34th G8 Summit. The Toyako Summit Memorial Museum keeps those days alive. It's a surreal feeling standing in front of the original round table and picturing Angela Merkel sitting right here, debating with George W. Bush or Dmitry Medvedev. Back in July 2008, the place must have been swarming with security details and journalists. Today, the exhibition feels almost intimate. Seeing that photo of Merkel—still relatively fresh in office back then (well, three years in)—stirs up memories of a bygone political era. It's a quiet space to reflect on how decisions made at tables like this steer the world—or don't—while outside, the volcano unbothered just keeps doing its thing.

# Conclusion: Sometimes Less to Sea (or Lake)

My departure tomorrow on December 22 feels both too soon and just right. I didn't do the hike I had planned. I didn't feel the shattered roads of Nishiyama Crater crunch beneath my boots. Instead, I experienced Toyako with a raw honesty that summer tourists probably never get to see.

It was a stay of quiet notes—not ticking off sights, just waiting and watching. And Japanese cuisine—thankfully far removed from any rice-pudding experiments—did the rest to keep morale high. The volcano isn't going anywhere. It's just waiting for my next visit, ideally when the ground actually has enough grip for my hiking boots.
