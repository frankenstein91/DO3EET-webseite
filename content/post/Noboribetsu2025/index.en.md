From December 22 to 24, 2026, I ventured straight into "Hell"—specifically, Noboribetsu Jigokudani in Hokkaido. Anyone who knows me knows I was actually hoping for a white Christmas. Hokkaido is world-famous for its powder snow, after all. Yeah, no such luck. Instead of fluffy fresh powder, I got a harsh dose of reality: everything was covered in sheer ice. Whatever snow had fallen had melted and refrozen into one giant skating rink. Without spikes under your boots, every single step was a minor gamble—but more on that in a moment.

Even so, it was a brilliant trip—above all thanks to the onsen culture, which I indulged in to the absolute fullest.

# The "Onsen Hack": Adex Inn
I stayed at the Adex Inn, which honestly turned out to be a stroke of genius. The hotel itself is fairly modern and practical, but the killer feature is the perk you get as a guest: free access to the massive "Grand Bath" at the Dai-ichi Takimotokan right across the street.

The Takimotokan is pretty much the flagship resort in town, sitting right at the edge of Hell Valley. Staying at the Adex Inn, you just walk across the road (don't forget your jacket in that freezing cold!) and get to soak in the exact same luxury pools as guests paying triple the price. It felt like I was over there multiple times a day. The view from the main bath looking straight out at the steaming vents is simply unbeatable.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/Noboribetsu2025/Blick von Hölle auf Takimotokan.jpg" alt="View from the onsen toward Hell Valley" width="1400" height="1400" >}}

What impressed me most, though, was the sheer number of pools. I think there are five different types of spring water there. The water smells and feels different in each one—sometimes sulfur (the classic: smells like rotten eggs, great for your skin), sometimes iron or salt. The temperatures vary wildly too. There are pools you can lounge in forever, and others where you feel like you're about to be boiled alive. That variety is incredible; you can basically go on a "bath-hopping tour" without ever leaving the building.

## Quick Onsen Guide: Buckets and Etiquette

In case you've never been to an onsen—or need a refresher—an onsen isn't just a swimming pool. Around here, it's practically a religion. So you don't embarrass yourself at the Takimotokan, here are the golden rules:

1. Naked is mandatory: Swimwear is strictly forbidden in the actual onsen areas. You leave everything in the locker. Don't worry, nobody stares. It's completely natural.
2. The traditional washing ritual: I found this especially fascinating. Alongside the regular showers, there's an area where you wash the old-fashioned way. You sit naked on tiny stools around an extra-small basin and scoop water with little buckets to pour over yourself. It feels very primeval, even though the buckets here were curiously made of plastic rather than wood. Still, there's something deeply meditative about it. Only once you're squeaky clean are you allowed into the main pools.
3. The small towel: You're given a small towel. You can use it for a bit of modesty while walking around, but it must never touch the bathwater! Most Japanese guests fold it up and balance it on top of their heads. It might look funny, but it's super practical.  

I loved just lying in the piping-hot water while everything outside was frozen solid. In the outdoor area at Takimotokan, you're even allowed to drink alcohol (there's a little service window for it). I stuck with a salted lemonade.

# Slip-and-Slide to the River Oyunuma
One of my absolute highlights was definitely the River Oyunuma Natural Footbath—a natural footbath tucked away in the forest where the hot river flows right through.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/Noboribetsu2025/natürliches Fußbad.jpg" alt="The natural footbath at River Oyunuma, where you can soak your feet in the warm river." width="1400" height="1400" >}}

The hike there, however, was... an adventure. Because it hadn't snowed and everything had frozen into solid ice, the trail was insanely slick. I nearly went flying more than once. If you visit in winter when there's no fresh snow: watch your step! Still, the effort paid off. You sit on wooden planks in the middle of the woods, pull off your boots and socks, and dangle your feet in the warm, gray river. The riverbed is natural, so a bit muddy, but the sensation is unbeatable: ice-cold air on your face and toasty-warm feet. One tip: be sure to bring your own towel to dry off—there are none provided.

# Why I Skipped the Bear Park
You may have heard of the Noboribetsu Bear Park up on the mountain. I know plenty of tourists go there, but I made a conscious choice to skip it and want to explain why. From an animal welfare perspective, I find the conditions there genuinely troubling. The bears are often kept in concrete pits, begging tourists for food. To me, that has nothing to do with experiencing nature, and I didn't want to fund it with my admission ticket. Instead, I chose to enjoy the wild nature down in the valley. It just feels right.

# The Magic of the Evening
One last insider tip before wrapping up: Noboribetsu is packed during the day. But in the evening, once all the day-tripper tour buses have cleared out (especially the huge groups of Chinese tourists), a wonderful quiet settles over the town.

That's when I often headed back out. The wooden boardwalk into Hell Valley (Jigokudani) is illuminated at night—they call it "Onibi no Michi" (Path of the Demon Fire). Steam hisses into the air, lights reflect off the ice, and you practically have this surreal landscape all to yourself. That's the moment when you truly feel the raw power of the volcanoes.

So if you ever find yourself out this way: check into the Adex Inn, skip the bear show, and enjoy the hot springs (and the little buckets!) at Takimotokan. It's worth every yen!
