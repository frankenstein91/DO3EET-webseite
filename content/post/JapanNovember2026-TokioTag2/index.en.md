Day two in Tokyo was all about fascinating contrasts: from the early-morning industrial vibe of the new Toyosu Fish Market to the colorful hustle of historic Tsukiji, two masterfully crafted Edo gardens tucked amidst Minato's skyscrapers, and finally the breathtaking backdrop of Tokyo Bay.

A day packed with minor technical absurdities—from losing a ticket lottery amid CVE advisories and accidental detours through restricted wholesale zones to a monumental Ghibli clock stuck in maintenance mode—capped off by walking across the Rainbow Bridge and watching the spectacular new water show *Tokyo Aqua Symphony*.

<!--more-->

---

## Early Morning in Toyosu: Tuna Auction and CVE Bad Luck

The alarm rang mercilessly early: if you want to watch Tokyo's fishmongers at work, you have to be up before sunrise. Thankfully, staying right in Toyosu meant only a short walk to the wholesale market that opened in 2018.

Anyone who remembers the old Tsukiji market gets hit with serious culture shock in Toyosu: no labyrinthine wooden halls, no slippery alleys splashed with ice water—instead, a gigantic, ultra-modern building complex that looks more like a high-security logistics hub or a pharmaceutical plant. Everything is climate-controlled, regimented, and strictly divided into functional zones.

For the world-famous morning **tuna auction** (*Maguro no Seri*), there is an official lottery to watch the spectacle from a special lower viewing deck right at floor level. This time I didn't get lucky in the draw—though the rejection arrived in remarkably nerdy fashion:

The operators had abruptly put their official web portal into maintenance mode after security vulnerabilities came to light (specifically CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619). The website itself was completely locked down for queries—yet the backend still ran reliably enough to drop my electronic "better luck next time" straight into my inbox right on schedule. You really have to admire Japanese thoroughness.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag2/PXL_20261109_210701137.jpg" alt="Frozen tunas lined up for the morning auction at Toyosu Fish Market" width="600" height="1067" >}}

The auction action was still great to watch from the regular second-floor visitor gallery: through double-paned glass you look down onto the vast auction floor of *Suisan-Oroshiuri-ba* (the seafood wholesale area). Laid out on large blue tarps, the frozen giants lie neatly in rank and file. Every tuna bears white chalk markings listing weight, origin, and lot number. Each tail end has a clean triangular cross-section cut out so intermediate wholesalers (*nakaoroshi*), armed with hooks and flashlights, can meticulously inspect fat content, marbling, and meat color before the auctioneers' rapid-fire chants knock each lot down.

Staring at multi-hundred-kilo prized fish all morning inevitably works up an appetite—and the reward followed immediately: for a well-earned market breakfast, I had freshly landed tuna two ways—wonderfully aromatic grilled tuna and, of course, melt-in-your-mouth tender sushi. You can hardly eat fresher or closer to the source in Tokyo!

## Odyssey on Bus 市01: The Google Maps Wholesale Market Trap

After touring the market, it was time to head over to the traditional old market district of **Tsukiji**. A quick check on Google Maps promised the perfect, effortless connection: hop on Toei Bus **route 市01** (Ichiba 01) toward Shimbashi Station, departing every 10 minutes right from *Suisan-Nakaoroshi-Tō* (Wholesale Building) and taking just 12 minutes across five stops to *Tsukiji-Rokuchōme*.

Sounds simple—except on the ground it turned out to be a genuine navigation trap:

What Google Maps conveniently neglected to mention was that this listed stop sits deep inside the restricted logistics compound accessible exclusively to authorized dealers, delivery trucks, and market staff!

Instead of a normal bus shelter, I suddenly found myself facing a massive locked security gate. While trying to reach the stop via an alternate street-level approach, alert security guards stopped me not once, but twice. With utmost politeness—yet unmistakable hand gestures—they made it clear that as a tourist I had wandered straight into a strictly off-limits zone. After a bit of hunting around, I eventually tracked down the regular public stop outside the facility perimeter—and the 市01 rolled up right on time.

## Tsukiji: Lively Outer Market and the Stone Temple of Hongwanji

After the quick hop across the bay, the bus dropped us off at *Tsukiji-Rokuchōme*—and the contrast with Toyosu couldn't have been sharper:

While the wholesale trade moved to Toyosu, the **Tsukiji Outer Market** (*Jōgai Shijō*) is still bursting with life. In the narrow alleys, locals and visitors jostle between street stalls, traditional Japanese knife shops, and tiny food stands. Everywhere something is steaming or sizzling: the aroma of freshly grilled scallops, oysters, dashi broth, and freshly rolled, slightly sweet *tamagoyaki* (Japanese omelet) hangs in the air. Here you can feel Tokyo's classic market heartbeat up close.

Just steps away from the market stalls rises one of the capital's most unusual religious landmarks: **Tsukiji Hongwanji** (築地本願寺).

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag2/PXL_20261109_231910567.jpg" alt="Monumental Indian-Buddhist stone facade of Tsukiji Hongwanji Temple" width="1200" height="675" >}}

This Jōdo Shinshū temple breaks radically with the look of traditional Japanese wooden architecture. After the original historic structure was destroyed in the Great Kantō Earthquake of 1923, architect Chūta Itō designed a monumental replacement out of solid stone, drawing heavily on ancient Indian temples and Buddhist cave monasteries (such as Ajanta). With its sweeping front staircase, stone elephants and lions, and lotus-shaped canopies, it looks like a building dropped in from another world—a striking oasis of calm right on the edge of the bustling market.

## Urban Oases in a Light Drizzle: Hama-rikyū & Kyū-Shiba-Rikyū

From Tsukiji, I walked on toward Shiodome and Hamamatsuchō, home to two of the city's most significant historic gardens. The morning brought a light, misty drizzle—not a downpour, just that gentle kind of rain that muffles city traffic and makes natural colors pop.

### Hama-rikyū Gardens: Open Space and Tidal Ponds

**Hama-rikyū Gardens** (*Hama-rikyū Onshi Teien*) overwhelms first and foremost with sheer scale. Covering more than 25 hectares, the former hunting and villa grounds of the Tokugawa shogunate sit right where the Sumida River meets the bay.

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag2/PXL_20261110_010342291.jpg" alt="Fresh red camellia blossom with glistening raindrops in Hama-rikyū Gardens" width="600" height="1067" >}}

Along the winding paths, early red camellias (*tsubaki*) were blooming, morning dew and raindrops glistening on their petals. From the eaves of the wooden rest pavilions, ornate Japanese rain chains (*kusari-doi*, 鎖樋) trickled softly:

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag2/PXL_20261110_014620659.jpg" alt="Japanese rain chain kusari-doi with cup-shaped bowls on a wooden pavilion" width="600" height="1067" >}}

The centerpiece of the grounds is *Shioiri-no-ike*—Tokyo's only remaining seawater tidal pond, whose water level rises and falls with the bay's tides via a sluice gate. Out in the middle of the water, reached across curved wooden bridges, perches the historic *Nakajima no Ochaya* teahouse:

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag2/PXL_20261110_022054151.jpg" alt="Nakajima no Ochaya teahouse on the tidal pond of Hama-rikyū Gardens" width="1200" height="675" >}}

### Kyū-Shiba-Rikyū: Intricate Miniature Art Between Train Tracks

Just one train stop further south in Hamamatsuchō waits **Kyū-Shiba-Rikyū Garden** (*Kyū Shiba Rikyū Onshi Teien*). Where Hama-rikyū was all about expanse and breathing room, Kyū-Shiba-Rikyū impresses with masterful compression:

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag2/PXL_20261110_033857916.PANO.jpg" alt="Panorama of Kyū-Shiba-Rikyū Garden showing the pond, sculpted pines, and surrounding skyscrapers" width="1200" height="370" >}}

The garden is far more compact, yet extraordinarily intricate: every single pine tree has been shaped and propped with tremendous patience and precision, and boulders hauled from across Japan are stacked into miniature mountain ranges. Winding around the central pond is a classic stroll garden (*kaiyūshiki-teien*) sitting in sharp, almost surreal contrast to the skyscrapers towering right next door and Yamanote Line trains rushing past.

## Shiodome & the Ghibli Clock: When the Gears Stay Silent

In the early afternoon, we headed back over to Shiodome to the NTV Tower (*Nippon Television*). Mounted on the exterior facade on the second floor hangs a genuine work of art: the monumental 18-meter-wide steampunk clock **Nittele Dai-Dokei** (日テレ大時計), designed by Studio Ghibli legend **Hayao Miyazaki**.

Hammered from over 1,200 hand-worked copper plates, this gigantic mechanical contraption immediately evokes the fantastical worlds of *Howl's Moving Castle*. Normally, the clock springs to life at set times: countless gears, bellows, boilers, and mechanical figures move in sync to custom-composed orchestral music.

Right on time at 14:55, we stood expectantly in front of the facade to catch the 15:00 show. The hands clicked onto three, anticipation peaked—and nothing happened. Not a chime, not a gear turning, dead silence.

The explanation eventually turned up in the fine print of NTV's official Japanese website: tucked into half an A4 page of notice text, the broadcaster stated that mechanical shows and figure animations were currently suspended for scheduled inspection and maintenance. Oh well—the structure remains a breathtaking piece of art even at rest, and an unexpected punchline is simply part of any proper day of exploring! Inside the adjacent NTV lobby, a quick detour to Studio Ghibli's most famous forest spirit offered a nice consolation prize.

Proper afternoon refueling came shortly thereafter courtesy of a culinary highlight of a different kind: at Freshness Burger, they were serving the limited-edition **神の海老バーガー ゆず白味噌** (*Kami no Ebi Burger Yuzu Shiro Miso*—"God Shrimp Burger with Yuzu and White Miso"). Three crisply fried premium prawns paired with mild Kyoto white miso, fresh shiso leaf, and bright yuzu citrus zest: a sensational Japanese spin on a gourmet burger!

## Crossing the Rainbow Bridge on Foot & Tokyo Aqua Symphony in Odaiba

Late afternoon brought a true highlight that plenty of Tokyo visitors miss out on: crossing the bay to Odaiba—not on the automated Yurikamome line, but **all the way on foot across the Rainbow Bridge**!

Via the *Rainbow Promenade* (the pedestrian walkway running along the lower deck of the suspension bridge), you stroll roughly 1.7 kilometers above the water. Along the northern route, the view opening up toward the heart of the metropolis is nothing short of breathtaking:

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag2/PXL_20261110_080714863.jpg" alt="View from the Rainbow Bridge across the Minato skyline and glowing Tokyo Tower at dusk" width="1200" height="675" >}}

As twilight settled in, the sky flushed soft purple while ships traced paths far below and office towers in Minato flickered to life. Right in the middle of it all: the red-and-white glow of Tokyo Tower, standing like the city's familiar lighthouse.

Arriving in Odaiba, we headed straight down to the sandy beach of *Odaiba Marine Park*. Waiting there was Tokyo's newest attraction: **Tokyo Aqua Symphony** (東京アクアシンフォニー), which opened in spring 2026.

Anchored roughly 170 meters offshore is a gigantic sea-based water organ whose jets shoot up to 150 meters high, making it one of the largest water shows in the world. The performance blends dynamic water choreography, towering fountains, colored lasers, and LED projections synced tightly to well-known music tracks. An impressive spectacle right out on the waves of Tokyo Bay!

And the perfect finale to this jam-packed day was the nighttime view back across the sheltered bay:

{{< imgwebp src="https://do3eet-media.dreamofjapan.de/posts/JapanNovember2026-TokioTag2/PXL_20261110_085020264.jpg" alt="The Rainbow Bridge illuminated in blue at night with traditional yakatabune boats on Tokyo Bay" width="1200" height="675" >}}

The Rainbow Bridge glowed in deep, vivid blue while strings of skyscraper lights shimmered across the dark water. Between them glided unhurried traditional *yakatabune* (Japanese houseboat dinner cruisers), their red and gold paper lanterns painting warm streaks of light across the waves.

A day split between a high-tech wholesale market, tranquil garden artistry, silent clockwork quirks, and sweeping cityscapes comes to a close. Tokyo finds a new way to wow you every single day!
